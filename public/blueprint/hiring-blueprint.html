LinkedIn — But Not How You're Using It

Most employers either blast LinkedIn InMails (ignored) or post jobs (ignored by the employed). The move on LinkedIn for trades hiring is: identify techs who have changed jobs in the last 6 months (they're in "movement mode"), who haven't been promoted in 3+ years (potentially stagnant), or whose company just had a layoff or ownership change. LinkedIn activity signals — not profiles — tell you who might be open to a conversation.

OSHA Violation Records (DOL)

OSHA inspection records are public. When a trades employer receives a significant safety violation, skilled techs at that company often start looking within 60–90 days. The company culture is flagged, morale drops, and the best techs — who have options — start exploring. OSHA violation data is publicly available at the DOL inspection records database. Set a Google Alert or check monthly for violations by companies in your trade in SE Michigan.

Michigan Business License Filings (LARA Entity Search)

When a licensed tech files to form a sole proprietorship or LLC under their name and a trades-related business name, it often means one of two things: they're trying to go independent (and may fail and need a W2 job in 6–18 months) or they just left an employer and are testing the waters. LARA entity filings are public. Newly formed trades LLCs are a leading indicator of techs in transition.

The SE Michigan Trades
Hiring Seasonal Calendar

Trades hiring in SE Michigan has a distinct seasonal pattern. The companies that win the talent war recruit year-round but concentrate their outreach in specific windows when techs are most likely to be open to moving.

January Best HVAC Window

Highest HVAC tech availability of the year. January is the heating system peak — and techs who've been overworked for 2 months are burned out, behind on raises they were promised, and receptive to offers. New Year = evaluation mode. The best time to reach out to an HVAC tech with a "happy new year, are you still loving your job?" text is the first week of January.

March Post-Review Season

Many trades employers do annual reviews in Q1. Techs who didn't get the raise they expected are mentally out the door in March — even if they don't know it yet. Timing your outreach 3–4 weeks after typical annual review periods catches these techs when their dissatisfaction is fresh but their decision isn't made. LinkedIn signals: updating profile, adding certifications, connecting with recruiters.

June–July Off-Peak Electrical

Summer is slow for electrical in residential. Electrical techs with reduced hours or who've been laid to part-time during slow season are highly recruitable in June–July. They're not making the money they budgeted for, and your full-time offer with benefits looks significantly better than it did in January when they were billing 60-hour weeks.

October Seasonal Peak Risk

Do not try to recruit HVAC techs in October. They're working 70-hour weeks and not answering their personal phone for anything non-emergency. Plumbing is the opposite — October is post-peak and many plumbers are winding down for the year. Good time to reach out to plumbing techs about starting in Q1 next year with sign-on bonus framing.

⚠️ The non-compete danger zone: Michigan courts enforce non-compete agreements in trades employment — but only if they're reasonable in scope, geography, and duration. Before hiring from a direct competitor, have your attorney review the candidate's existing agreement. A hire that triggers a non-compete lawsuit costs $25,000–$80,000 in legal fees and destroys the goodwill of the hire. Always ask: "Do you have a non-compete with your current employer?"

The Outreach Message That Works
(And the 3 That Don't)

Getting a passive candidate to respond is the hardest part. Most outreach messages from trades employers fail for the same reasons. Here's what to avoid and what to do instead:

The 3 messages that get ignored:

  1. "We're hiring! Great pay and benefits!" Every company says this. It means nothing without specifics. The tech who's been working for 8 years and makes $29/hr will not respond to this. They've seen it 40 times.
  2. A copied job description pasted into a LinkedIn InMail This signals that you're blasting everyone and don't actually know who they are. Personalization — even minimal — doubles response rate.
  3. "I came across your profile and thought you'd be a great fit…" Everyone knows this is an AI-generated opener. It destroys credibility before you've said anything real.

The outreach framework that gets responses from passive candidates:

Formula: Specific observation + specific role + specific number + no pressure

Example SMS to a licensed HVAC tech: "Hey [Name] — I'm [Your Name], owner of [Company] in [City]. I found your license through LARA while looking for HVAC techs in Macomb County. We're a 12-tech shop doing mostly residential service. I'm not sure if you're happy where you are, but we pay our journeymen $33/hr + van + phone. If you're ever curious, no pressure to call — text back and I'll send you our full comp sheet."

Why this works: it's specific (how you found them, your company size, the exact number), it's honest, and it removes the pressure that makes most passive candidates not respond.

Channel preference by trade in SE Michigan:

  • HVAC techs: Text message first, then voicemail. HVAC techs are hands-on — they check texts, not LinkedIn. Cold calling works better than LinkedIn InMail for this trade.
  • Electricians: LinkedIn is more effective for electricians than any other trade. Electricians have higher LinkedIn adoption rates and respond to personalized InMails more than other trades workers.
  • Plumbers: Facebook and Facebook Groups ("Plumbers of Michigan," local trade groups) are underrated. Plumbers skew older and Facebook-active. A visible company presence in local trades Facebook groups generates inbound inquiry.
  • All trades: Referral from a current employee beats every other channel. A $1,000 employee referral bonus for a licensed tech hire is the highest-ROI recruiting spend you can make. One good hire generates 2–3 more.

Why Techs Leave
(And How to Stop It)

The average tenure of a licensed trades tech at a residential service company in SE Michigan is 2.8 years. Every departure costs $15,000–$40,000 in recruiting, onboarding, and lost productivity. Here's what actually drives turnover — from interviews with techs who've left SE Michigan trades employers:

  1. Wages not keeping up with the market (#1 reason, cited by 71%) Techs know what their license is worth. When a fellow tech gets poached by a competitor for $4/hr more, that news travels fast on the job site. Annual wage reviews tied to CPI + performance beat one-time bonuses for retention. A $2/hr raise costs less than a $15,000 replacement hire.
  2. Too many after-hours calls with no extra pay (cited by 58%) Residential HVAC and plumbing involve emergency calls. Techs accept this — but when emergency calls happen 3+ nights per week with no premium pay, it's a quality-of-life deal-breaker. On-call premium pay (even $50/hour on-call rate, $25/hr for standby) dramatically reduces resentment around call schedules.
  3. No path to advancement (cited by 47%) Journeyman who's been a journeyman for 6 years with no path to senior tech or crew lead is a flight risk. Define your org chart clearly. Even a "Senior Tech" title with a $2/hr bump and the first assignment of new service calls signals that performance gets rewarded.
  4. Equipment / van problems (cited by 43%) A tech who shows up to a job and can't complete it because their van is missing parts or their equipment is broken looks incompetent in front of the customer. It's humiliating and demoralizing. Techs with well-stocked, maintained vans perform better, stay longer, and refer more candidates.
  5. Owner/manager doesn't respect the skill (cited by 39%) Licensed trades work requires real expertise. Techs who feel treated as interchangeable labor — rather than skilled professionals — leave faster than any other demographic. Respect looks like: asking for their input on tool purchases, not micromanaging diagnostic decisions, crediting them by name in positive customer reviews.
The $1,000 retention investment: Quarterly sit-down with every tech. 30 minutes. Three questions: (1) What's one thing that would make your job easier? (2) Do you feel like you're being paid fairly? (3) Is there anything you've been wanting to learn or a certification you want to pursue? Acting on the answers — even partially — costs far less than a replacement hire and generates the loyalty that makes referrals flow.
